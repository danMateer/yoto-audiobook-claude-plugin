# Story craft

How to get from a one-line premise to a manuscript worth narrating. The pattern here
came out of a 12-chapter, 14,440-word book that survived four specialist review
passes and several generalist ones.

## The brief

Draft a one-paragraph brief and get agreement before writing 14,000 words. Decide
these yourself and state them in the brief rather than asking:

- Chapter count and length. Twelve chapters at roughly 1,200 words each is a
  read-aloud shape: about six minutes of narration per chapter, one bedtime.
- Chapter titles as beats. A title is a promise about what changes.
- Third person, past tense, one narrator.

Ask about these, because getting them wrong wastes the whole draft:

- **The cast, if they are established characters.** A reader who knows them will
  catch a wrong voice in one line. Get the tics, the relationships, and who is
  allowed to be competent.
- **The age band.** An 8-year-old primary audience is a different book from a
  5-year-old one at the sentence level, not just the vocabulary level.
- **Whether older listeners get served too.** A parent or a 13-year-old sibling in
  the room changes the joke budget. See the dual-audience section.
- **The emotional throughline.** One character learns one thing. Which character,
  and what.

## Structure that holds up

**One emotional line, running under the plot.** The plot is what happens. The line is
what one character is carrying. In the fishing book the plot was a fishing trip and
the line was Donald's inability to sit with wanting something badly. Every chapter
advanced both, and the ones that only advanced the plot got rewritten.

**The moral is never stated out loud.** No character explains the lesson, and the
narrator never summarizes it. Show it landing through an action instead. Daisy put
her hand on Donald's arm and said nothing about patience; that beat did the work a
paragraph of explanation would have wrecked. If you can delete a sentence and lose
nothing but the lesson, delete it.

**Interior state lives in narration, not dialogue.** Children's characters who
announce their feelings read as instructional. Put the feeling in what the character
notices, or in what they do with their hands.

**The supporting cast gets an arc too, usually guilt.** When the protagonist struggles
and the friends laugh, the friends noticing they laughed is the second-best beat in
the book. It gives the resolution somewhere to land.

**Running gags escalate on a schedule.** Establish, repeat with a variation, then pay
off changed. A gag that repeats identically three times is filler. One that escalates
is structure.

**Chapter endings alternate.** Some on a laugh, some on a small ache, some on a
question. Three laughs in a row flattens the book.

## Dual-audience humor

The primary band gets physical comedy, repetition, silly names, and character logic
played straight. The older band gets three to five bits per book that work on a second
level: an absurd formula delivered with total confidence, a deadpan line that is funny
because of what it refuses to say, a piece of bureaucratic or scientific parody.

Two rules keep this from turning into winking:

1. **The elevated joke must also work at the primary level**, as pure silliness. A
   professor surfacing from a submarine in a pond to deliver an equation is funny to
   an 8-year-old because a submarine is in a pond. The equation is for the 13-year-old.
2. **Never signal it.** No nudge, no aside to the adult reader. The joke pays or it
   does not.

Suppressed fury is the highest-value register in this kind of book and the easiest to
get wrong. `"NOTHING."` from a character who is plainly furious is funnier than any
shouted line, and it needs the narration to stay out of the way completely.

## Reviewer briefs

Run four specialists in parallel, read-only, then revise yourself. Give each one the
full manuscript and one job. Reviewers that get two jobs return mush.

**Character consistency.** Is every character recognizably themselves in every line?
Verbal tics present without becoming a tic count? Anyone competent who should not be,
or foolish who should not be? For established characters, name every line that a
long-time reader would reject.

**Comedy and humor.** Which jokes land, which are limp, which repeat without
escalating? Is the joke density even across chapters, or does the middle sag? Are the
elevated bits actually elevated, and do they still work as plain silliness? Flag every
joke that requires knowing something the primary audience will not know.

**Story and moral.** Is the emotional line continuous, chapter by chapter? Is the
lesson ever stated out loud, and if so where? Does the resolution earn itself, or does
a character simply change? Is anyone let off the hook too easily?

**Narrative structure.** Pacing chapter to chapter, scene transitions, whether any
chapter could be cut without loss, whether the climax has enough room, whether the
read-aloud rhythm breaks anywhere. Flag every paragraph that is hard to say out loud.

Then generalist passes: read the whole thing for flow, fix the seams the specialists
opened, and repeat until a fresh read turns up nothing structural. Two or three passes
is normal. Stop when the notes become preferences rather than problems.

## Writing for a voice, not a page

The manuscript is going to be read aloud by a model, so:

- Dialogue attribution stays simple. A narrator who reads "he expostulated" reads it
  exactly that badly.
- Long sentences need a place to breathe. Read every paragraph out loud yourself; the
  ones you stumble on are the ones the model will stumble on.
- Emotional direction written as narration will be spoken, not performed. "said Von
  Drake sadly" is a line to read. If you want the sadness performed, that is what an
  audio tag is for, and the narration should not also say it.
- Numbers spelled as words. The QC pass compares transcripts, and a model that reads
  "406" correctly still writes it back as digits.
- Paragraph breaks are pause instructions. The renderer packs chunks on paragraph
  boundaries and the model puts about 1.1 seconds at each one.
