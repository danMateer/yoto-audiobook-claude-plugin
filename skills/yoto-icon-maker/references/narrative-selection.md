# Narrative selection

Use this guide when mapping audiobook chapters to icon subjects.

## Recognition hierarchy

A useful Yoto chapter icon answers two questions quickly:

1. What story world or character is this?
2. What makes this chapter different from adjacent chapters?

The first answer normally comes from a face or landmark. The second comes from one expression, accessory, prop, or color cue.

## Face-first selection

Choose a character face when the chapter contains a clear viewpoint character, emotional center, introduction, confrontation, or comic reaction. Faces work well because children distinguish species, hair, ears, head shape, eyes, and expression even when detail is sparse.

Rank face candidates by:

1. narrative importance in this chapter;
2. recognizability at 16x16;
3. distinctness from nearby icons;
4. availability of one chapter-specific expression or identifier.

Use frontal or three-quarter views. Profiles consume too much of the grid unless the nose, muzzle, beak, or hat is the character's defining silhouette.

For a recurring character, create a stable base model:

- same head shape and ear or hair silhouette;
- same core colors;
- same eye construction;
- same outline treatment.

Then vary only what belongs to the chapter: mood, hat, tiny held object, foreground badge, or one secondary face. Avoid redesigning a character from chapter to chapter.

## Landmark selection

Choose a landmark when arrival, travel, discovery, or setting is the narrative beat. Good landmarks have a silhouette that survives without labels: one tower, dome, arch, bridge, mountain, tree, vehicle, or skyline rhythm.

Reduce a city to two or three height bands plus one signature structure. Reduce an interior to one dominant object or architectural shape. Use a bright rim or window pattern so the shape separates from the black device display.

Do not use a generic landscape when a character face would better identify the chapter.

## Object and action selection

Use an object when it functions as a chapter token: a crown, key, carrot, letter, train, lantern, suitcase, or musical instrument. The object should occupy most of the canvas.

Use an action only if it can be expressed by two large shapes. Examples include a face beside a microphone, a hand holding a key, or a boat beneath a wave. Avoid crowds, chase scenes, fights, dances with full bodies, or rooms with several props.

## Spoilers and age appropriateness

Prefer a chapter's setup, focal character, or early memorable image over a late reveal. If the only distinctive motif is a spoiler, use a neutral face, landmark, or object associated with the chapter.

Keep frightening imagery symbolic and readable rather than graphic. Match the tone of the source and the likely child audience.

## Ambiguity test

Before approving a mapping, describe the icon in five words or fewer. If the description needs multiple clauses, simplify it.

Ask: "If the label vanished, could a listener who knows the story identify this chapter or at least its focal character?" If not, choose a stronger motif.
